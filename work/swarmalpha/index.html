How do partial views
become a collective decision?

SwarmAlpha built an external observation and experiment layer to study how information moves through a group of AI agents—and where its decisions change.

The artifact

SwarmAlpha studies collective decision processes: what agents see, how their reports change through discussion, and what an external observer can measure. Controlled interventions make parts of that larger question testable.

The work produced an experimental pipeline, historical comparisons, and a multi-channel process descriptor with explicit measurement contracts. Code, experiment records, and negative results remain available; active research has stopped and the archive is retained for engineering handoff.

Observe → Experiment → Revise

Observe. The early question concerned heterogeneous agents with local information. An observation layer outside their discussion sought to make group states explicit and comparable.

Experiment. A narrower question became testable: hold the discussion starting point fixed, then vary the information disclosed. ATTACKS had lower average Brier loss than SUPPORTS, while individual tasks showed the reverse.

Revise. Separate failure-prediction and cross-channel audits did not establish the stronger predictive claims. Later work sharpened a multi-channel description of information exposure, explicit use, source records, responses, and resources—keeping description, prediction, and guidance separate.

This is a retrospective thread through a changing research programme. The implemented descriptor has synthetic structural checks; empirical validity and general governance remain unresolved. Inspect the descriptor contract, prediction audit, and cross-channel audit.

A finding worth keeping. A question still open.

The experiments branched from a shared discussion state and varied the information agents received: a baseline continuation, supporting relations, or attacking relations. They then compared final probability reports using Brier loss, where lower is better.

The 16 August 2026 V3 report records a post-hoc replication across 45 tasks and two seeds with deepseek-chat. ATTACKS − SUPPORTS averaged −0.412 Brier loss, with a reported 95% task-cluster bootstrap interval of [−0.571, −0.267]. Seed 0 reused the earlier run; seed 1 was new. Read the V3 report or the earlier one-seed report.

An average difference can exist before we know when to choose an intervention. A development-stage within-task association between evidence reuse and Brier loss did not replicate in V3’s confirmation set. A later, separate analysis also found that the ATTACKS advantage was not confined to initially wrong groups, narrowing an appealing “targeted rescue” interpretation. Read the revised interpretation.

Historical reported findings, not a new reproduction. Fixed arm order, missing reports, and insufficient re-presentation controls limit mechanism claims; complete V3 raw outputs are absent from public Git. The task is the statistical unit, with seeds averaged within each task. The later analysis reused V3 data alongside a separate GLM batch; it is not an independent replication of V3.

Separate the intervention from the explanation

Changing the information agents see can reveal a response without settling why it happened. The follow-up’s correction matters because an attractive “targeted rescue” story was narrower than the evidence allowed. Keeping the condition, measured output, and interpretation separate makes that correction visible.

I own the research question, experiment boundaries, and interpretation, with AI assistance in analysis, implementation, drafting, and checks. The historical reports preserve the decisions and their qualifications. Read the research identity and design philosophy.

What would be valuable next?

The next useful conversation is about the mechanism: which information, for which initial group state, under which matched conditions? The archive keeps the units, missing-data treatment, and interpretation available for that inspection.
