Why I’m building it

Questions about ambition, freedom, belonging, and how to live rarely begin with us. In The Ancestors, I bring earlier thinkers’ works and disagreements into a collection organized around those enduring questions. Reading paths connect different answers while distinguishing comparison from documented historical influence.

Alongside these histories, I preserve questions I have asked and a record of how my own judgments change. The collection already contains exhibits, source records, and routes through related questions. Its immediate purpose is personal growth; over time, I want it to remain something others can read, question, and build on.

Am I building the life I want—or the life others have taught me to admire?

You can work hard, make the expected choices, and become someone others admire. Then a quieter doubt appears: is this a life you want to live, or one you have learned to want? A course of study, a career, even an ordinary day can carry that uncertainty.

The first difficulty is that admiration can reward appearances. Adam Smith distinguishes receiving praise from deserving it. In The Theory of Moral Sentiments, approval based on a mistaken picture cannot offer the same satisfaction as conduct that merits approval, even unseen. This shifts the question from how a life appears to what would make it worthy of esteem. Read Part III, chapter I.

Yet a goal can survive that test and still feel strangely unchosen. Harry Frankfurt distinguishes a desire that occurs in us from one with which we identify. His account of reflection asks which motives we are prepared to accept as our own. Wanting something and wanting it to guide a life are different questions. Read Taking Ourselves Seriously, printed pages 172–175.

Reflection still leaves a life to be lived. John Stuart Mill argues that choosing a plan of life exercises judgment and character; simply following custom does not exercise those capacities in the same way. His experiments of living leave room to discover what suits a person, provided those experiments do not harm others. Read On Liberty, chapter III.

These works do not offer one shared answer. Smith examines deserved esteem; Frankfurt examines our relation to our motives; Mill defends individuality and choice. Read together, they leave a more precise question: What would I still choose, what reasons could I stand behind, and what might I learn only by trying?

This reading path brings public works into conversation. Smith: the 1777 Dublin text, Part III, chapter I. Frankfurt: the 2004 Stanford Tanner lectures, printed pages 172–175. Mill: On Liberty, chapter III, Project Gutenberg’s Walter Scott edition. The connections are my editorial framing, rather than claims of historical influence.

A collection I return to

The thinkers matter as people with works, historical circumstances, independent positions, and limits. Their disagreements give me reasons to reconsider a question, rather than a single answer to retrieve. I choose the questions and make the lasting judgments; AI helps with searching, checking sources, and organization in the background.
