A map to grow with.

Questions about ambition, freedom, belonging, and how to live rarely begin with us. The Ancestors is my personal database of intellectual lineages: a way to place those questions on a map of human thought, with earlier thinkers’ works, lives, and disagreements still in view.

Its present purpose is personal growth. Returning to a question with a different experience can change what a book means—and reveal how much an earlier answer left out. The aim is to build a long-term cognitive infrastructure from those connections.

Am I building the life I want—or the life others have taught me to admire?

A course of study, a career, a way of spending the day: a choice can look admirable before we know whether we want to live it. It is easy to borrow a picture of success. Harder to ask what, in that picture, is worth keeping.

Adam Smith makes room between receiving praise and deserving it. In The Theory of Moral Sentiments, approval based on a mistaken picture cannot offer the same satisfaction as conduct that merits approval, even unseen. This shifts the question from how a life appears to what would make it worthy of esteem. Read Part III, chapter I.

Yet a goal can survive that test and still feel strangely unchosen. Harry Frankfurt distinguishes a desire that occurs in us from one with which we identify. His account of reflection asks which motives we are prepared to accept as our own. Wanting something and wanting it to guide a life are different questions. Open the official lecture page and text: Taking Ourselves Seriously, sections 2–6.

Reflection still leaves a life to be lived. John Stuart Mill argues that choosing a plan of life exercises judgment and character; simply following custom does not exercise those capacities in the same way. His experiments of living leave room to discover what suits a person, within the rights and interests of others. Read On Liberty, chapter III.

These works do not offer one shared answer. Smith examines deserved esteem; Frankfurt examines our relation to our motives; Mill defends individuality and choice. Read together, they leave a more precise question: What would I still choose, what reasons could I stand behind, and what might I learn only by trying?

An editorial reading route composed for this page from public original works, not a historical dialogue or an export of private notes. Smith: the linked 1777 Dublin text, Part III, chapter I. Frankfurt: Stanford Tanner lectures delivered in 2004, Taking Ourselves Seriously. Mill: the 1869 fourth-edition text hosted by Econlib, chapter III. The questions connecting them are the framing of this page.

Keep the disagreement alive.

A map of thought becomes useful when a familiar question opens onto another person’s work, circumstances, and different answer. The aim is to stay in contact with those differences as understanding changes.

I choose the questions and return to the sources. AI helps with retrieval, comparison, drafting, and organization in the background; responsibility for the durable judgments remains mine. The personal collection remains private.

When should an anomaly change an explanation?

A prediction fails. Should we question the observation, revise the model, or change the research programme?

The testable claim

Karl Popper

A claim must risk an observable failure. Accepting a falsification also requires a testable refuting effect; a stray contradictory observation alone does not settle the case.

The Logic of Scientific Discovery, chapter 4, sections 19–22

The research programme

Imre Lakatos

Evaluate the sequence of revisions: does it lead to new predictions that later receive support, or only accommodate facts already known? A young programme may need time; that is not unlimited protection.

The reasoned choice

Thomas S. Kuhn

Researchers can share values such as accuracy and scope while applying and weighting them differently. The absence of a compulsory choice algorithm does not make every choice equally defensible.

Where the answers diverge

These are different units and purposes of evaluation: a testable refutation, the progress of a programme, and reasoned choices within a community. The reading path does not fuse them into one verdict procedure.
